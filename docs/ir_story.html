Topic 1

Industrial relations: concepts and approaches

Industrial relations (IR) is the study of the relations between workers, employers and the State at work. This topic covers what IR means, the main approaches and thinkers, the concepts of labour, the aims and causes of poor IR, and how market forces now shape IR.

Law today Concept topic. No Code section applies; the ideas are unchanged by the Codes.
  1. Dunlop (1958): an IR system has four parts: actors, contexts, ideology and a body of rules.
  2. Pluralism: both parties strive for, and have the opportunity to exercise, economic and political power.
  3. Citizen concept: labour has a right to be consulted on its terms and conditions of work.
  4. Tripartism: government, employers and workers act as "equal and independent partners".
  5. Market forces: IR is now more influenced by external market forces than by the power play between employers and employees.

1. Meaning, actors and tripartism

ILO glossary (2005): IR covers the individual and collective relations between workers and employers, at work and arising from the work situation. It also covers the relations between their representatives at industry and national levels, and their interaction with the State.

Dale Yoder: IR is the "whole field of relationship that exists because of the necessary collaboration of men and women in the employment process of an industry". In another wording: "relationships between management and employees or among employees and their organizations, that characterize or grow out of employment".

Tripartism

Tripartism is "the active interaction of government, employers and workers (through their representatives) as equal and independent partners in efforts to seek solutions to issues of common concern" (ILO glossary, 2005).

2. Dunlop's systems approach

John T. Dunlop published "Industrial Relations Systems" in 1958. He said an IR system has four parts.

1. Actors

A hierarchy of managers and their representatives in supervision. A hierarchy of workers (non-managerial) and any spokesmen. Specialised governmental agencies (and specialised private agencies created by the first two actors).

2. Contexts

The technological characteristics of the workplace and work community. The market or budgetary constraints on the actors. The locus and distribution of power in the larger society.

3. Ideology

It binds the system together.

4. Rules

A body of rules.

3. Approaches to IR

ApproachCore ideaUnions and power
UnitaryThe system is in basic harmony. Conflict is "unnecessary and exceptional". Unions are seen as intrusions from outside. Management prerogative is legitimate and accepted.
PluralisticThe organisation is made of individuals who form separate sectional groups, each with its own interest, objectives and leadership. It is managed through a variety of roles, institutions and processes. Collective bargaining is a political institution and a power relationship between employers and employees. Unions balance the power between management and employees. Both parties strive for, and have the opportunity to exercise, economic and political power.
Marxist or radicalClass conflict arises from the disparity in the distribution of, and access to, economic power. Economic power is unevenly distributed. Exploitation continues unabated under pluralist institutions. Marxists are the major critics of the pluralist approach.

The Textile Labour Association (TLA), Ahmedabad, was founded in 1920 by Anasuya Sarabhai and Mahatma Gandhi.

Names tied to pluralism

  1. Alan Fox: linked to the pluralist frame and often called a "radical pluralist". He later wrote a radical critique of pluralism (1974, 1979).
  2. Allan Flanders (1970): set out a pluralist approach to IR, known as the Oxford approach. He wrote: "a system of industrial relations is a system of rules" and "the study of industrial relations may therefore be described as a study of job regulation".
  3. H.A. Clegg: grouped with Flanders as a main architect of British IR institutionalism. He wrote "Pluralism in industrial relations" (British Journal of Industrial Relations 13(3), 1975).

Other approaches

  1. Social action (Weber): gives weight to control under increasing rationalisation and bureaucratisation. The actors' understanding of the work situation matters more than social factors.
  2. Human relations (Elton Mayo): developed from the Hawthorne experiment at the Western Electric plant, which ran from 1924 to 1932. Informal groups set informal rules on workers.
  3. Psychological and socio-ethical: the socio-ethical approach is not much widely accepted.
  4. Human resource management (HRM) is also named as an approach.

4. Concepts of labour

5. Objectives and causes of poor IR

Objectives of IR

  1. Industrial peace: avoid industrial conflict, especially work stoppages, go-slow, gheraos, strikes and lockouts.
  2. Industrial democracy: establish and maintain it, based on the participation of labour in the management and gains of industry.
  3. Higher productivity: facilitate production and productivity, for example by reducing labour turnover and absenteeism.
  4. Better economic condition of workers.

Related policy aims

Indian labour policy has two-fold objectives: industrial peace and the welfare of labour. Mutual settlement of disputes through collective bargaining and voluntary arbitration is encouraged as a tenet of the National Commission on Labour's IR policy.

6. Industrial democracy, the Webbs and F.W. Taylor

Industrial democracy and the Webbs

The term comes from Sidney and Beatrice Webb, "Industrial Democracy" (London, 1897). For the Webbs it means trade-union self-government and common-rule bargaining, not workers' sharing in managerial decisions.

The term is used in other senses too.

  1. Indian IR use: "government mandated worker participation at various levels of the organisation with regard to decisions that affect workers". "Workers share in decision making" holds only for this sense.
  2. ILO: the term is used loosely. One view applies it only to powers that counter-balance management.
  3. Another account: it denotes the democratic organisation of unions and collective bargaining.

F.W. Taylor

  1. 1911: scientific management needs "a complete revolution in the mental attitude and the habits of all of those engaged in the management, as well of the workmen" (Principles of Scientific Management).
  2. 1912: Taylor told the House Special Committee it involves "a complete mental revolution" on the part of the workingman and "the equally complete mental revolution" on the part of management.
  3. The exact phrase "mental revolution" is from the 1912 statement, not the 1911 book. It is a scientific-management idea.
  4. Functional management: it replaces the single foreman by eight different men, each with his own special duties. Four are in the planning room. Four are shop bosses: gang bosses, speed bosses, inspectors and repair bosses.

7. IR and market forces

IR is now more influenced by external market forces than by the power play between employers and employees.

Globalisation has made competition so imperative that stoppage tactics, such as stopping production, no longer hold good.

  1. Market forces and workforce changes have placed an immense burden on trade unions (Sridharan, 2017).
  2. The ILO Committee of Experts says sympathy strikes "are becoming increasingly frequent because of the move towards the concentration of enterprises, the globalization of the economy and the delocalization of work centres" (Gernigon, Odero, Guido, 1998).
  3. A study of 15 industrialised countries links globalisation with declining strike rates, though not in a steady line.

Topic 2

Trade unions

How a group of workers becomes a union the law recognises, protects and must bargain with. We follow one union through its whole life.

Madras, 1918

Workers at the Buckingham and Carnatic Mills form the Madras Labour Union, one of India's first modern unions. Its leader is B.P. Wadia. When the union strikes, the mill owners go to the Madras High Court.

In 1920 the court orders the leaders to stop. Persuading workers to break their contracts is a civil wrong, it says. A union can now be sued out of existence.

N.M. Joshi takes the fight to the Central Legislative Assembly. The answer is the Trade Unions Act 1926, with a simple bargain: register your union, and the law shields it.

A century later that bargain is still the core of the law. Only the statute has changed.

Law today IR Code Ch III (IR Code ss.5 to 27), in force from 21 November 2025 (S.O. 5320(E)). It replaced old TU Act 1926, repealed from the same date IR Code s.104(1).

If you remember only five things

  1. 7 members apply to register a union IR Code s.6(1). At least 10% of the workers or 100, whichever is less, must be members IR Code s.6(2).
  2. Registration is optional: members "may apply" IR Code s.6(1).
  3. With more than one registered union, 51% of the workers on the muster roll makes a sole negotiating union IR Code s.14(3). If no union has 51%, a negotiating council is formed from unions with at least 20% IR Code s.14(4).
  4. A registered union is a body corporate IR Code s.12. Members must be 14 or more and employed in a non-hazardous industry IR Code s.20. Office-bearers must be 18 or more IR Code s.21(1)(i).
  5. The right to strike is a common law right, not a fundamental right. The Code regulates strikes IR Code ss.62, 63.

Our example: Shakti Mills

Shakti Mills is an imaginary textile mill with 1,100 workers. Five groups of workers want to form unions: A, B, C, D and E. We will follow them through every chapter.

Chapter 1 Form: what counts as a union

IR Code s.2(zl) Trade Union

Any combination, temporary or permanent, formed primarily to regulate relations between workers and employers, workers and workers, or employers and employers.

It also covers a combination formed to impose restrictive conditions on the conduct of any trade or business.

It includes any federation of two or more Trade Unions.

old TU Act 1926 s.2(h) the same definition. Only the wording changes: "workmen" became "workers" Changed

What is a union for? Four answers

Thinkers disagree on the purpose. Their answers run from "fix today's pay" to "end the wage system".

Improve working lives

Webb

"A continuous association of wage earners for the purpose of maintaining or improving the conditions of their working lives" (History of Trade Unionism, 1920 edition). In Industrial Democracy, unions sit in "the industrial democracy of the future". The earliest use of "collective bargaining" they knew was by Beatrice Potter in 1891.

Overthrow the system

Marx

Unions as "a lever for the final emancipation of the working class", that is "the ultimate abolition of the wages system" (Value, Price and Profit).

Guard scarce jobs

Perlman

A Theory of the Labor Movement: American unionism stabilised when scarcity consciousness replaced abundance consciousness. Conservative leaders strove harder for full job control than their radical rivals.

It depends on the union

Hoxie

Five functional types: business, friendly or uplift, revolutionary, predatory and dependent. Evolutionary unionism is not one of them.

Chapter 2 Register: the numbers test

At Shakti Mills

Seven workers of group E meet in the canteen and sign an application. That is enough people to apply. But is it enough people to register? The Registrar checks a second number.

IR Code s.6(1) Who applies

Any 7 or more members of a trade union may apply for registration. Registration is optional: the Code says "may apply".

old TU Act 1926 s.4(1) any 7 or more members; also "may apply" Same
IR Code s.6(2) Strength on the date of application

The members must be at least 10% of the workers or 100, whichever is less.

old TU Act 1926 s.4 first proviso: the same test Same

Which Shakti Mills unions pass?

10% of 1,100 is 110, but 100 is less, so the threshold is 100. Unions with 100 or more members qualify.

B: 370 members, qualifies

Unions A, B, C and D qualify. The Code IR Code s.6(2) uses the same test as the old Act, so the result is the same.

Group E had its 7 signatures. It still fails, because 80 is below 100. 7 opens the door; 10% or 100 lets you in.

IR Code s.6(4) Strength after registration

The union must at all times keep at least 10% of the workers or 100, whichever is less, subject to a minimum of 7.

old TU Act 1926 s.4 second proviso: not less than 7 members on the date of application Changed

The old Act checked once, at the door. The Code keeps checking. If union C (110 members) later loses a dozen members, it falls below the line.

Why the law counts "outsiders"

Many of India's best-known union leaders never worked in the factory they led. They were lawyers, doctors and politicians. Datta Samant, who led the Bombay mill strike of 1982, was a medical doctor. Outsiders brought education and political weight. They also brought party rivalries into the workplace. So the law caps them.

Outside leadership has a historical background and lingered after independence. Illiteracy, backwardness and fatalism of workers contributed to dependence on it.

IR Code s.23(2) Outsiders in the executive

All office-bearers except not more than one-third of them, or 5, whichever is less, must be persons actually engaged in the establishment or industry. A retired or retrenched employee is not an outsider.

old TU Act 1926 s.22(2) the corresponding rule, with the same Explanation on retired or retrenched employees
IR Code s.23(1) Outsiders, unorganised sector

Not less than one-half of the office-bearers must be persons actually engaged in the industry.

old TU Act 1926 s.22(1) the corresponding rule

Both outsider rules sit in IR Code s.23. "One-half" applies only to unions in an unorganised sector, which the appropriate Government notifies. "One-third or 5" is the general rule.

Chapter 5 Bargain: who sits at the table

The old problem

For decades, no central law told an employer which union to talk to. A firm could favour a friendly union and ignore the rest. Rival unions fought for the employer's ear, sometimes violently. The Code ends the guessing with a counting rule.

The Code requires a negotiating union or a negotiating council in an industrial establishment that has a registered Trade Union IR Code s.14(1).

How many registered unions are in the establishment?

One The employer recognises it as the sole negotiating union, on prescribed criteria IR Code s.14(2) needs rules. It does not need 51%.
More than one The share of workers on the muster roll, verified as prescribed, decides IR Code s.14(3) needs rules. See the two cases below.

51% or more: that union is the sole negotiating union IR Code s.14(3). No union at 51%: a negotiating council is formed IR Code s.14(4), with seats only for unions that have 20% or more of the workers.

The council has representatives of unions that have the support of not less than 20% of the workers on the muster roll. It has one representative for each 20%, and for the remainder after calculating the membership on each 20%.

The second National Commission on Labour (2002) recommended that a system of recognising the negotiating agency be placed on the statute. The Code now does it IR Code s.14.

IR Code s.27(1) needs rules Central Trade Union recognition

Where the Central Government is "of the opinion that it is necessary or expedient", it may recognise a Trade Union or federation as a Central Trade Union Organisation (CTUO), in the prescribed manner.

In 1946 the Government verified the membership of AITUC and IFL. This is a past event, not a Code rule.

Union security: how much must a worker do?

Once a union wins, it wants members to stay and pay. Union security arrangements sit on a scale, from "join before you are hired" to "nothing at all".

Two more arrangements sit beside the scale. Preferential shop: management agrees to give union members the first chance of employment. Check-off: the employer deducts union dues from pay and passes them to the union.

India: the second National Commission on Labour (2002, para 6.73) recommended compulsory check-off in establishments of 300 or more workers. It is a recommendation, not law.

Closed shop and union shop both force membership. The difference is timing: closed shop before hiring, union shop after probation. Agency shop forces dues, not membership.

Chapter 6 Strike: the last weapon

Two strikes that shaped the law

Bombay, January 1982. About 2.5 lakh textile workers walk out under Datta Samant. The strike drags on for over a year. Many mills never reopen.

Tamil Nadu, July 2003. State government employees strike. The government dismisses about 1.7 lakh of them. The case reaches the Supreme Court as T.K. Rangarajan.

Is striking a right? And if it is, can the law put limits on it?

Is it a right? The courts' answer

  1. The ConstitutionConstitution Art 19(1)(c) gives citizens the right "to form associations or unions or co-operative societies".
  2. 1961: All India Bank Employees' Association v National Industrial Tribunal (28 August 1961, AIR 1962 SC 171). The right to form unions under Constitution Art 19(1)(c) does not carry a right to strike. The right to strike or to lock-out may be controlled or restricted by appropriate industrial legislation.
  3. 2003: T.K. Rangarajan v Government of Tamil Nadu (6 August 2003). Employees have no fundamental right to strike. There is no fundamental, legal or equitable right to go on strike. The Court also said there is no legal or statutory right to strike. The case was about government employees only.

The right to strike is a common law right, not a fundamental right. So Parliament may regulate it, and the Code does.

The Code's rules

IR Code s.2(zk) Strike

A cessation of work by a body of persons employed in any industry acting in combination, or a concerted refusal to work. It includes concerted casual leave on a given day by 50% or more of the workers.

So a "mass casual leave" by half the workforce on one day is a strike in law.

IR Code ss.62, 62(1) Notice and bars

No person in an industrial establishment may strike, in breach of contract, without notice within 60 days before striking.

Other bars: within 14 days of the notice; before the date in the notice; during conciliation and for 7 days after; during Tribunal proceedings and for 60 days after; during notified arbitration and for 60 days after; and while a settlement or award applies.

IR Code s.63(1)(i) Illegal strike

A strike started in breach of IR Code s.62 is illegal.

Problems of Indian unions

Multiplicity

Many unions is the rule in India, and rivalry causes violent incidents. The second National Commission on Labour (2002) describes the movement as marked by multiplicity of unions, fragmentation and politicisation.

Outside leadership

See Chapter 4. Rooted in history; capped by IR Code s.23.

Weak coverage

The same Commission says working-class organisations are weakened by fragmentation, disenchantment and poor unionisation.

Data (Labour Bureau, 2018): average membership per registered union was 2,537. Only 4,771 unions (7.2%) filed returns.

Topic 3

Collective bargaining, grievance and discipline

Collective bargaining is how employers and workers' organisations settle terms of work by negotiation. This topic covers who negotiates with the employer, how a worker's grievance is heard, and how misconduct is inquired into and punished.

Law today IR Code s.14 (bargaining), IR Code s.4 (grievance) and IR Code s.38 (suspension), in force from 21 November 2025 (S.O. 5320(E)). They replaced old TU Act 1926, old IESO Act 1946 and old ID Act 1947, repealed from the same date IR Code s.104(1). The Industrial Relations (Central) Rules 2026 (G.S.R. 342(E), 8 May 2026) and the Model Standing Orders 2026 (S.O. 2312(E), 8 May 2026) are in force on publication. The Code of Discipline is voluntary, not law.
  1. With more than one union, the union with 51% of the workers on the muster roll is the sole negotiating union IR Code s.14(3). If none has 51%, a negotiating council forms from unions with at least 20% IR Code s.14(4). Recognition lasts 3 years, up to 5 in all IR Code s.14(6).
  2. A Grievance Redressal Committee is needed where 20 or more workers are employed. It has equal members, a cap of ten (old Act: six), and a chair that rotates every year IR Code s.4.
  3. A worker goes to the conciliation officer within 60 days, through the Trade Union the worker belongs to IR Code s.4(8).
  4. A suspended worker's inquiry is completed ordinarily within 90 days IR Code s.38(1). Subsistence allowance is 50% for the first 90 days and 75% after, if the delay is not directly attributable to the worker's conduct IR Code s.38(3).
  5. The Giri approach favours arbitration, with courts and tribunals as a last resort. The Code of Discipline (1958) is an "essentially moral agreement", not a statute.

1. Collective bargaining

Meaning

ILO Convention No. 154, Article 2, treats collective bargaining as all negotiations between an employer, a group of employers or employers' organisations on one side, and one or more workers' organisations on the other.

The purposes are to determine working conditions and terms of employment, to regulate relations between employers and workers, and to regulate relations between employers (or their organisations) and a workers' organisation.

Beatrice Webb used the term "collective bargaining" in her 1891 book The Co-operative Movement in Great Britain. Say "used", not "coined".

Features commonly listed: bipartite, continuous, flexible, a group action, and a process of give and take. Its outcome is a collective agreement.

2. Bargaining under the Code

The Code gives a statutory route to bargain: a negotiating union or a negotiating council, as the case may be.

IR Code s.14(1) needs rules The route

An establishment bargains through a negotiating union or a negotiating council. The matters for negotiation are "as may be prescribed".

old TU Act 1926 had no statutory recognition of a negotiating union. The Code introduces it New
IR Code s.14(2) Only one union

Where only one registered union functions, the employer recognises it as the sole negotiating union, on prescribed criteria.

IR Code s.14(3) More than one union

The union with 51% or more of the workers on the muster roll, verified as prescribed, is the sole negotiating union.

IR Code s.14(4) No union has 51%

A negotiating council is formed of unions with at least 20% support. It has one representative for each 20% and for the remainder after counting each 20%.

IR Code s.14(5) Council agreement

An agreement is reached if the majority of the union representatives agree.

IR Code s.14(6) Validity

3 years from recognition or constitution, or a further period the employer and union mutually decide, up to 5 years in all.

The facilities for the union are "as may be prescribed" IR Code s.14(7) needs rules. The National Commission on Labour (2002) had recommended placing recognition on the statute.

Central sphere only

These rules cover Central-sphere establishments only, in force on publication.

IR Central Rules r.9(2) Single union

Where only one registered union operates and has at least 30% of the total workers, the employer recognises it as the sole negotiating union.

IR Central Rules r.9(3) Verification of membership

The Central Government appoints a verification officer to verify union membership. The employer bears the expense. The officer meets the employer and the unions to decide the process of verification by secret ballot. The employer may use an electronic process.

Under the proviso at IR Central Rules r.9(3)(a), the process starts three months before the existing recognition period expires.

IR Central Rules r.9(1) Matters for negotiation

They include classification of grades and categories, orders under the standing orders, wages (wage period, dearness allowance, bonus, increment and allowances), hours of work and shifts, leave and holidays, promotion and transfer policy and disciplinary procedures, quarter allotment, safety, health and working conditions, other conditions of service, and any other matter agreed.

3. The Giri approach and the voluntary codes

The Giri approach

V.V. Giri was President of India from 24 August 1969 to 24 August 1974. The Giri approach is described as that of "a firm believer in negotiation and voluntary conciliation" who "hates compulsory arbitration". Courts and tribunals are a last resort. The answer to learn is arbitration, meaning voluntary arbitration or mutual settlement.

Dated steps

  1. Oct 1957 The tripartite Standing Labour Committee (16th session) adopts the Code of Discipline, with certain modifications.
  2. May 1958 16th Indian Labour Conference, Nainital, 19 and 20 May 1958. The Code of Discipline gets its 1958 form here. The Model Grievance Procedure is evolved at the same session. The Inter-Union Code of Conduct is among its papers: learn "1958".
  3. Nov 1962 Industrial Truce Resolution, adopted by a joint meeting of the central organisations of employers and workers, during an emergency.
  4. 21 Nov 2025 IR Code s.14 in force: the statutory route to a negotiating union.

The 15th ILC was at New Delhi on 11 and 12 July 1957. The 16th was at Nainital in 1958, not 1957.

What the Code of Discipline says

It is an "essentially moral agreement". Its sanctions are internal to its own machinery, not legal penalties. It has no statutory effect under the IR Code.

  1. No unilateral action by either side on any industrial matter.
  2. Use the grievance procedure.
  3. Recognise unions.

It sets recognition criteria of 15% of the workers in the establishment (plant level) and 25% of the workers of the industry in the local area (industry level).

4. Grievance

Meaning

Jucius defines a grievance as any discontent or dissatisfaction, whether expressed or not, whether valid or not, arising out of something connected with the company that an employee thinks, believes or feels is unfair, unjust or inequitable. The IR Code does not define "grievance".

The handling models are the open-door type and the step-ladder type.

Model Grievance Procedure (1958)

It was evolved at the 16th ILC in May 1958. It is a model procedure, not a statute.

  1. Worker to the officer designated by management The answer is due within 48 hours.
  2. Worker to the head of department The answer is due within 3 days.
  3. Worker to the Grievance Committee The Committee makes its recommendations to the manager within 7 days of the worker's request.
  4. Management gives its final decision The period is 3 days from receiving the Committee's recommendations.
  5. Voluntary arbitration The union and the management jointly may refer the grievance within a week of the worker receiving management's decision. It is not the worker's own right.

Students confuse the Committee's period with management's period because both are short. The Committee has 7 days. Management has 3 days, not 7.

Domestic inquiry

The clause numbers below are from Schedule A (mining), Model SO 2026 Schedule A cl.24.

  1. Employer suspends The worker may be suspended pending investigation or enquiry into complaints or charges of misconduct Model SO 2026 Schedule A cl.24(1). Subsistence allowance is paid on the condition that the worker takes no employment elsewhere during the suspension Model SO 2026 Schedule A cl.24(3).
  2. Statement of reasons The employer gives the worker a statement setting out in detail the reasons for suspension within a week of the suspension Model SO 2026 Schedule A cl.24(6)(b).
  3. Enquiry The worker may appear in person, or be represented by an office-bearer of a Trade Union of which he is a member, or by a co-worker of his choice Model SO 2026 Schedule A cl.24(6)(c). The clause does not mention lawyers. The proceedings are recorded in Hindi, English or the language of the State, whichever the charged worker prefers Model SO 2026 Schedule A cl.24(6)(d).
  4. Time The enquiry proceedings are completed within 90 days. The enquiry officer may extend the period for reasons recorded in writing Model SO 2026 Schedule A cl.24(6)(e).
  5. Finding and penalty If the worker is found guilty, he gets a reasonable opportunity to make a representation on the proposed penalty. The employer then passes an order of dismissal, suspension, fine, stoppage of annual increment or reduction in rank, if it considers that penalty meets the ends of justice Model SO 2026 Schedule A cl.24(6)(f). The authority weighs the gravity of the misconduct, the worker's antecedents and any extenuating or aggravating circumstances. A copy of the order goes to the worker Model SO 2026 Schedule A cl.24(7).
  6. Effect on pay On dismissal, the worker is treated as absent for the suspension and gets no remuneration for it. The subsistence allowance already paid is not recovered. On fine, stoppage of increment or reduction in rank, the worker is treated as on duty and gets wages less the subsistence allowance paid. If not guilty, the worker is deemed on duty for the suspension and gets the same wages as if not suspended, less the subsistence allowance paid Model SO 2026 Schedule A cl.24(6)(g).
  7. Appeal The worker may appeal within 21 days of receiving the order to the appellate authority the employer specifies. That authority hears the worker and passes an order within 15 days of receiving the appeal Model SO 2026 Schedule A cl.24(8).

Students merge the two 90-day clocks because both are 90 days. The enquiry officer's limit is 90 days, extendable for recorded reasons Model SO 2026 Schedule A cl.24(6)(e). Separately, Model SO 2026 Schedule A cl.24(2) asks for investigation and enquiry together, ordinarily within 90 days of suspension.

The Model Standing Orders list five penalties and do not label them minor or major. Those labels come from Government service rules.

Topic 4

Industrial disputes and their settlement

This topic is about what an industrial dispute is, who is covered, how a dispute travels from the shop floor to a Tribunal, and the rules for strikes, lock-outs, lay-off, retrenchment and closure. It also covers the penalties for breaking these rules.

Law today The Industrial Relations Code, in force from 21 November 2025 (S.O. 5320(E)). It repealed old ID Act 1947 from the same date IR Code s.104(1). Old Tribunals and authorities keep working until the matching Code bodies are functional IR Code s.104(1A). A Code Tribunal exists only once the appropriate Government notifies it IR Code s.44(1).
  1. Industrial dispute means employer v employer, employer v worker or worker v worker IR Code s.2(q). An individual worker's dispute over discharge, dismissal, retrenchment or termination is deemed an industrial dispute IR Code s.4(9). The worker may go to the Tribunal after 45 days IR Code s.4(10) and within 2 years IR Code s.4(11).
  2. Strike notice binds every industrial establishment: give it within 60 days before striking, and do not strike within 14 days of it IR Code s.62(1).
  3. Machinery: conciliation officer, then a two-member Tribunal IR Code s.44(2), and a National Industrial Tribunal for national matters IR Code s.46. There is no Board of Conciliation and no Court of Inquiry.
  4. Prior permission for lay-off, retrenchment and closure applies at 300 or more workers, or a higher number the Government notifies IR Code s.77(1). It was 100 workmen old ID Act 1947 Ch VB.
  5. Closure notice is 60 days in the ordinary case IR Code s.74(1), or an application for permission 90 days before in Chapter X IR Code s.80(1).

1. Industrial dispute

IR Code s.2(q) Industrial dispute

A dispute or difference between employers and employers, employers and workers, or workers and workers.

It must be connected with employment or non-employment, the terms of employment, or the conditions of labour, of any person.

New limb: it also includes a dispute between an individual worker and the employer connected with, or arising out of, discharge, dismissal, retrenchment or termination of that worker.

old ID Act 1947 s.2(k) the same three pairings and the same subject matter, with no individual limb Changed

2. Industry and the triple test

IR Code s.2(p) Industry

Any systematic activity carried on by co-operation between an employer and a worker (employed directly or through any agency, including a contractor) for the production, supply or distribution of goods or services to satisfy human wants or wishes, not being wants merely spiritual or religious.

It is an industry whether or not capital has been invested, and whether or not the activity is carried on for gain or profit.

old ID Act 1947 s.2(j) repealed. The Code text follows the 1982 text of that section, which the Court said was never notified Changed

Four exclusions

  1. Charitable, social or philanthropic institutions, owned or managed by organisations wholly or substantially engaged in such service.
  2. Sovereign functions of the appropriate Government. This includes all activities of Central Government departments dealing with defence research, atomic energy and space.
  3. Domestic service.
  4. Any other activity the Central Government notifies.

The triple test (Rajappa, 1978)

In Bangalore Water Supply and Sewerage Board v. A. Rajappa (decided 21 February 1978 by a seven-judge Bench; 1978 AIR 548 = (1978) 2 SCC 213) the Supreme Court said an enterprise is prima facie an "industry" where there is:

1. Systematic activity

The work is organised and regular.

2. Co-operation

It is organised by co-operation between employer and employee.

3. Goods or services

It produces or distributes goods and services to satisfy human wants and wishes, not spiritual or religious ones.

  • Absence of a profit motive or gainful objective is irrelevant, whether the venture is public, joint, private or other.
  • Professions, clubs, educational institutions, co-operatives, research institutes and charitable projects cannot be exempted if they meet the triple tests. Small exceptions exist for simple ventures with no real employees and for eleemosynary missions.
  • Only sovereign functions "strictly understood" are exempt. Welfare activities of economic ventures run by Government are not (the dominant nature test). Chandrachud J, in a separate opinion, would not except sovereign functions at all.
  • Hospitals: the Court approved State of Bombay v. Hospital Mazdoor Sabha (1960). It overruled other decisions such as Cricket Club of India and Madras Gymkhana.
  • The triple-test opinion is the lead opinion of Krishna Iyer J, for himself, Bhagwati J and Desai J. The other judges wrote separately.

Students say Rajappa overruled Hospital Mazdoor Sabha because a common explanation sits next to the answer. The judgment says the opposite: it approved that case.

Under the Code, and the 2026 nine-judge bench

The three elements now sit in the text of IR Code s.2(p), and the case name still marks their source.

State of U.P. v. Jai Bir Singh 2026 INSC 897, decided 20 August 2026

A nine-judge bench reconsidered the triple test for "industry" under old old ID Act 1947 s.2(j). It wrote five separate opinions. This is a court statement on the old Act, not a Code rule.

  • CJI Surya Kant (for himself, Sharma, Aradhe and Pancholi JJ) said the triple test was "susceptible to further refinement" and "would have been more appropriately structured, hypothetically speaking" in reformulated terms (para 287(ii)). The reformulation is "no more than our considered opinion" and "not intended to displace the governing legal position in respect of pending proceedings" (para 287(iii)).
  • Matters pending under the ID Act "may be adjudicated in accordance with the Triple Test laid down in Bangalore Water Supply" (para 287(iii)).
  • The CJI opinion refrains from examining the IR Code definition (para 287(v)). Rajappa "shall not be regarded as the sheet anchor for the interpretation of the IR Code" (para 287(vi)). The ruling is confined to disputes under the ID Act (para 288).
  • Datta J (with Bhuyan J), para 76: "Bangalore Water Supply (supra) does not require reconsideration".

So the answer to "which case laid down the triple test" is still Rajappa. But IR Code s.2(p) is read on its own text.

5. Route of a dispute under the Code

An individual grievance starts at step 1 and reaches the conciliation officer at step 3 IR Code s.4(8). At step 4 the officer holds proceedings where a dispute exists or is apprehended, or a strike notice has been given IR Code s.53(1) needs rules.

  1. Worker to Grievance Redressal Committee (GRC) The aggrieved worker files within 1 year from the date the cause of action arises IR Code s.4(5) needs rules.
  2. GRC decides It may finish within 30 days of receiving the application IR Code s.4(6). It decides by majority view, but only if more than half of the worker members agree IR Code s.4(7).
  3. Worker to conciliation officer Within 60 days of the decision (or of the end of the 4(6) period with no resolution), the worker applies for conciliation through the Trade Union of which he is a member IR Code s.4(8) needs rules.
  4. Conciliation officer The report is due within 45 days of the start of proceedings, or within 14 days where a strike notice was received IR Code s.53(5). The parties may extend the time by written agreement, with the officer's approval. The officer cannot hold proceedings after 2 years from the date the dispute arose IR Code s.53(1).
  5. Outcome of conciliation Settlement: the officer sends a report and the memorandum of settlement signed by the parties to the appropriate Government IR Code s.53(3). No settlement: the officer sends the parties and the Government a full report, in electronic or other prescribed form, saying why it failed IR Code s.53(4).
  6. Tribunal A concerned party applies in the prescribed form within 90 days of receiving the failure report IR Code s.53(6) needs rules. An individual worker may instead apply directly after 45 days from the conciliation application IR Code s.4(10), and before 2 years from the termination IR Code s.4(11). This works only once a Tribunal is in place IR Code s.44(1).
  7. Award It is communicated to the parties and the appropriate Government IR Code s.55(2) and becomes enforceable on the expiry of 30 days from communication IR Code s.55(3).

Two other lanes: employer and workers may refer a dispute to voluntary arbitration by written agreement IR Code s.42(1). The Central Government may refer a dispute of national importance, or affecting establishments in more than one State, to a National Industrial Tribunal IR Code s.54(1).

14. Strike and lock-out: when they are barred

IR Code s.62(1) Strike bars

No person in an industrial establishment may strike, in breach of contract:

  1. without giving the employer notice of strike within 60 days before striking. This is a window in which the notice must be given. The Code does not say "60 days' notice".
  2. within 14 days of giving the notice.
  3. before the expiry of the strike date named in the notice.
  4. during conciliation proceedings before a conciliation officer, and for 7 days after they end.
  5. during proceedings before a Tribunal or NIT, and for 60 days after they end.
  6. during arbitration proceedings, and for 60 days after they end, where a notification has been issued under IR Code s.42(5).
  7. during any period in which a settlement or award is in operation, on the matters it covers.

15. Illegal strikes and lock-outs, and the old rule

  • IR Code s.63(1): a strike or lock-out is illegal if it is commenced or declared in contravention of IR Code s.62, or continued in contravention of an order under IR Code s.42(7).
  • IR Code s.63(2): one already in existence when the dispute is filed or referred is not illegal in its continuance, if it was lawful at the start and the continuance was not prohibited under IR Code s.42(7).
  • IR Code s.63(3): a lock-out declared because of an illegal strike, or a strike declared because of an illegal lock-out, is not illegal.
  • IR Code s.64: no person shall knowingly spend or apply any money in direct furtherance or support of any illegal strike or lock-out.
  • Penalty IR Code ss.86(13), 86(14): a worker who commences or continues an illegal strike faces a fine of Rs 1,000 to Rs 10,000 or imprisonment up to 1 month, or both. An employer who does so for an illegal lock-out faces a fine of Rs 50,000 to Rs 1 lakh or imprisonment up to 1 month, or both.

Topic 5

Workers' participation in management

Workers' participation in management means workers taking part in decision-making in the enterprise. This topic covers the constitutional directive, the levels and forms of participation, the Indian schemes in order, the 1990 Bill, the Ahmedabad experiment and the committees in the Codes.

Law today Constitution Art 43A is a Directive Principle in Part IV. It is not a statute, and the Codes do not change it. The only participation rule in the IR Code is the Works Committee IR Code s.3(1) needs rules, in force from 21 November 2025 (S.O. 5320(E)). It carries forward old ID Act 1947 s.3, repealed from the same date. The Safety Committee is in OSH Code s.22, in force from 21 November 2025 (S.O. 5321(E)).
  1. Article 43A is in Part IV (Directive Principles). The 42nd Amendment Act 1976 inserted it, in force from 3 January 1977. No court can enforce it Constitution Art 37.
  2. Five levels, in order: information sharing, consultative, associative, administrative, decisive.
  3. Three thresholds: Works Committee 100 or more IR Code s.3(1); shop and joint councils (1975) 500 or more; unit councils (1977) 100 or more.

"The State shall take steps, by suitable legislation or in any other way, to secure the participation of workers in the management of undertakings, establishments or other organisations engaged in any industry."

4. Fixed-term employment

The IR Code gives fixed-term employment a statutory home. Two Codes define it, and each carries part of the gratuity rule. The Code on Social Security is in force from 21 November 2025 (S.O. 5319(E)), and that includes its definition section.

IR Code s.2(o) Definition

Engagement of a worker on a written contract of employment for a fixed period.

Code on SS s.2(34) says nearly the same, for an "employee". It is in force.

old ID Act 1947 did not use the term "fixed term". The Code adds the named category New
IR Code s.2(o) proviso (a) Parity

Hours of work, wages, allowances and other benefits are not less than those of a permanent worker doing the same work or work of similar nature.

IR Code s.2(o) proviso (b) Proportional benefits

A fixed-term worker is eligible for all statutory benefits of a permanent worker in proportion to the period of service, even if the qualifying period is not met.

IR Code s.2(o) proviso (c) Gratuity after 1 year

Eligible for gratuity after 1 year of service under the contract. Code on SS s.2(34) has no one-year gratuity condition.

Code on SS s.53(1)(d) No 5-year wait

Termination of the contract period under fixed term employment is a gratuity event. The 5-year continuous service rule is not needed on expiry of fixed term employment (second proviso to Code on SS s.53(1)). This section is in force.

Code on SS s.53(2) Pro rata gratuity

Under the third proviso, gratuity for a fixed-term employee is paid on a pro rata basis. The main rate is 15 days' wages (or the number of days the Central Government notifies) for every completed year of service, or part of a year above 6 months.

IR Code First Schedule Standing orders

Item 1 lists fixed term employment among the classes of workers for standing orders.

Students put all of the gratuity rule in one Code because both Codes define the term in nearly the same words. The 1 year is in IR Code s.2(o). The pro rata rule is in Code on SS s.53(2). Code on SS s.2(34) names no period.

Students say "same wages as a permanent worker". The Code says not less than, so parity is the floor IR Code s.2(o).

Expiry is not retrenchment

IR Code s.2(zh)(iv) Retrenchment excludes expiry

Termination of service on completion of the tenure of fixed term employment is left out of "retrenchment".

IR Code s.70 (one month's notice or pay, compensation of 15 days' average pay per completed year, notice to Government) applies to a worker who is "retrenched" after 1 year or more of continuous service. So it does not apply when the term ends by completion. Early termination is not covered by clause (iv).

IR Code s.2(zh)(iii) also leaves out non-renewal of a contract on expiry, or termination under a stipulation in it. old ID Act 1947 s.2(oo)(bb), inserted in 1984, already did this Same

How fixed-term employment came in

  1. 2016 Ministry of Labour and Employment notification of 7 October 2016: fixed-term employment allowed in apparel manufacturing only.
  2. 2018 Notification of 16 March 2018 (G.S.R. 235(E)) removes the words "in apparel manufacturing sector". The category now covers all sectors.

Answers

  1. old TU Act 1926, old IESO Act 1946 and old ID Act 1947, repealed from 21 November 2025 IR Code s.104(1).
  2. 300 or more IR Code s.28(1). The old Act said 100 old IESO Act 1946 s.1(3).
  3. The draft is deemed certified IR Code s.30(5).
  4. No. Pay and benefits must be not less than a permanent worker's IR Code s.2(o). Gratuity is due after 1 year of service under the contract IR Code s.2(o); it is paid pro rata Code on SS s.53(2).
  5. No. Termination on completion of the tenure of fixed term employment is left out of "retrenchment" IR Code s.2(zh)(iv).
  6. 75% of the wages immediately before suspension for the remaining period IR Code s.38(3)(b). The first 90 days carry 50% IR Code s.38(3)(a).
  7. The Tribunal (Industrial Tribunal under IR Code s.44) IR Code s.37. The old forum was the Labour Court old IESO Act 1946 s.13A.
