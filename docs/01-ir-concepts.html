01 Industrial relations concepts, approaches and theories

CONCEPT TOPIC This is concept material. It carries no Code section and no in-force status. The three PYQ keys below (Q53, Q56 and Q65) are kept for their exam year. EXAM-CONVENTION marks a key that the publisher treats as the answer while the sources differ or are silent.

Snapshot (keep these five)

  1. Q53, pluralistic perspective, key (a). Both parties strive (and have opportunity) to exercise economic and political power. EXAM-CONVENTION The exam wording is not in any IR text opened. In the pluralist view, collective bargaining is a political institution and a power relationship.
  2. Q56, citizen concept, key (c). Labour has a right to be consulted on its terms and conditions of work. EXAM-CONVENTION A course text words it as a right "to have a voice in determining the rules and regulations under which they work".
  3. Q65, key (a). Both statements true, and II explains I. EXAM-CONVENTION No source ranks market forces above the power play between employers and employees.
  4. Dunlop, 1958, "Industrial Relations Systems". Four parts: actors, contexts, ideology, a body of rules.
  5. ILO tripartism: "equal and independent partners" in "the active interaction of government, employers and workers". The ILO glossary covers the interaction of the two sides with the State.

1. Pluralistic approach (Q53)

Pluralistic approach. The organisation is made of individuals who form separate sectional groups. Each group has its own interest, objectives and leadership. The organisation must be managed through a variety of roles, institutions and processes.

In this view, collective bargaining is a political institution. It involves a power relationship between employers and employees. Unions balance the power between management and employees.

Look-alikes (PYQ-EPFO-2017-Q53 options). Learn one detail for each row.

Approach View of conflict Role of unions Who holds power
Unitary (Q53 option b) The system is in basic harmony. Conflict is "unnecessary and exceptional". Seen as intrusions from outside. Management prerogative is legitimate and accepted.
Pluralistic (Q53 key a) Separate sectional groups, each with its own interest. They are managed through roles, institutions and processes. Unions balance the power between management and employees. Collective bargaining is a power relationship between employers and employees.
Marxist or radical (Q53 option c) Class conflict arises from the disparity in the distribution of, and access to, economic power. Not stated in the sources used here. Economic power is unevenly distributed. Exploitation continues unabated under pluralist institutions.
Trusteeship (Q53 option d) Strikes are "the last weapon in the armoury of industrial workers". Not stated in the sources used here. Employers should regard themselves only as trustees or co-owners.

Fox and the radical row. IGNOU calls Fox a "radical pluralist". Hodder (2024) notes that he later wrote a radical critique of pluralism (1974, 1979). The Marxists are the major critics of the pluralist approach.

Names tied to pluralism.

  • Alan Fox. Sources link him to the pluralist frame. No opened text shows Fox calling himself a pluralist.
  • Allan Flanders. IGNOU says he "enunciated a pluralist approach to IR" (1970). A second course text headlines "the pluralist theory of Flanders (1970) / Oxford model". The two texts may share a source.
  • H.A. Clegg. Clegg is grouped with Flanders as a main architect of British IR institutionalism (Cullinane, OUP 2014). He wrote "Pluralism in industrial relations", British Journal of Industrial Relations 13(3), 1975. No opened source calls Clegg a pluralist in his own words.

2. Citizen concept of labour (Q56)

Citizen concept. Workers are citizens of the industry in which they are employed. They are entitled to a right to have a voice in determining the rules and regulations under which they work. This is the wording of one course text (CCS University).

Exam key. Q56 key (c) says labour has a right to be consulted on the terms and conditions of work. EXAM-CONVENTION Sources differ: "right to be consulted" appears in a college text (Gopalan) only as a trade-union aim.

Look-alikes (PYQ-EPFO-2017-Q56 options and the other concepts of labour).

Concept One testable detail
Commodity Labour is affected by the law of supply and demand and is priced by it. Q56 option (b) words it as "Labour is affected by the law of demand and supply". University texts treat it as a historical view.
Machinery Employers "started to regard their employees largely as machines capable of a certain amount of out-put". This is one course text. "Cog in the machine" is Q56 option (d), not that text's term.
Partnership Workers are partners in the enterprise. They have mutual responsibilities and a share in the fruits of the joint endeavour. The CCS text records that some call this concept "fallacious".
Citizen (Q56 key c) A voice in the rules under which workers work.

3. Q65, assertion and reason: market forces and globalisation

The stem pairs two statements (wording shortened here). Statement (I): IR is now more influenced by external market forces than by the power play between employers and employees. Statement (II): globalisation has made competition so imperative that union tactics like stopping production no longer hold good.

  • Key (a) for EPFO-2012: both true, and (II) explains (I).
  • Statement (I) is EXAM-CONVENTION. No source ranks market forces above the power play. One journal article says market forces and workforce changes have placed an immense burden on trade unions (Sridharan, Christ University Law Journal 6(1), 2017).
  • Statement (II) is EXAM-CONVENTION. No source says stoppages "no longer hold good". The ILO Committee of Experts says sympathy strikes "are becoming increasingly frequent because of the move towards the concentration of enterprises, the globalization of the economy and the delocalization of work centres" (Gernigon, Odero, Guido, ILO, 1998). That line covers sympathy strikes only. One study of 15 industrialised countries links globalisation with declining strike rates, in a non-monotonic way. It is an abstract only, and not about India.

7. Also know

Meaning, actors and tripartism

  • ILO glossary (2005). IR covers the individual and collective relations between workers and employers at work and arising from the work situation. It covers the relations between their representatives at industry and national levels, and their interaction with the State.
  • Dale Yoder. IR is the "whole field of relationship that exists because of the necessary collaboration of men and women in the employment process of an industry". Another course text quotes him as "relationships between management and employees or among employees and their organizations, that characterize or grow out of employment". Both come from course texts. Yoder's book was not read. The sources give two wordings and no year.
  • Tripartism (ILO glossary, 2005). "The active interaction of government, employers and workers (through their representatives) as equal and independent partners in efforts to seek solutions to issues of common concern".

Dunlop's systems approach

John T. Dunlop published "Industrial Relations Systems" in 1958. An IR system has four parts.

  1. Actors: a hierarchy of managers and their representatives in supervision; a hierarchy of workers (non-managerial) and any spokesmen; specialised governmental agencies (and specialised private agencies created by the first two actors).
  2. Contexts: the technological characteristics of the workplace and work community; the market or budgetary constraints on the actors; the locus and distribution of power in the larger society.
  3. Ideology: it binds the system together.
  4. Rules: a body of rules.

Text version: actors, contexts, ideology, rules. These are IGNOU's quotes from Dunlop. The 1958 book was not read. Short forms such as "employers' associations" and "unions" are paraphrase, not Dunlop's words.

Other approaches

  • Oxford approach (Flanders). Flanders wrote: "a system of industrial relations is a system of rules" and "the study of industrial relations may therefore be described as a study of job regulation" (Cullinane, OUP Handbook, citing Flanders 1970, p.86). Quote Flanders' words. "Institutions of job regulations" is the IGNOU course text's wording.
  • Social action approach. IGNOU says "the social action approach of Weber" gives weight to control under increasing rationalisation and bureaucratisation. A second text says the actors' understanding of the work situation matters more than social factors. The Weber link comes from IGNOU only.
  • Human relations approach. Elton Mayo developed human relations theory from the Hawthorne experiment at the Western Electric plant. The study ran 1924 to 1932. Mayo "did not become involved until 1927" (OpenStax); other sources date his joining to 1928. Informal groups set informal rules on workers.
  • Gandhian or trusteeship approach. Employers should regard themselves only as trustees or co-owners. Strikes should be "the last weapon in the armoury of industrial workers". Gandhi advocated voluntary arbitration and mutual settlement of disputes. The Textile Labour Association (TLA), Ahmedabad, was "founded in 1920 by Anasuya Sarabhai and Mahatma Gandhi" (SEWA's history of its parent body).
  • Psychological, socio-ethical and HRM approaches. The LPU course text names the psychological and socio-ethical approaches and calls the socio-ethical one "not much widely accepted". IGNOU Unit 1 names the human resource management approach.

Objectives of IR (as listed in course texts)

Lists differ between texts.

  • Avoid industrial conflict, to ensure industrial peace. IGNOU words it as avoiding an unhealthy atmosphere, especially work stoppages, go-slow, gheraos, strikes and lockouts.
  • Establish and maintain industrial democracy based on the participation of labour in the management and gains of industry.
  • Higher productivity. IGNOU: "to facilitate production and productivity". INFLIBNET: raise productivity by reducing labour turnover and absenteeism.
  • Improve the economic condition of workers (LBRCE, LPU texts). "Workers' welfare" was not found in an objectives list.
  • Indian labour policy has two-fold objectives: industrial peace and the welfare of labour. That is a labour-policy aim, not an IR objective.
  • Mutual settlement of disputes through collective bargaining and voluntary arbitration is encouraged as a tenet of the National Commission on Labour's IR policy, as cited by IGNOU. It is a policy tenet, not a listed objective.
  • Taylor's "mental revolution". One Indian course text (LBRCE) lists "a complete mental revolution of workers and employees" as the main object of IR. It counts as an IR objective only in coaching lists and that one text.

Causes of poor industrial relations

Sources say "causes of industrial disputes". They do not say "causes of poor IR", except in the organisational row.

Group Cause
Economic Wages, bonus and dearness allowance. Only one source (IJLMH) names dearness allowance.
Managerial An authoritarian or autocratic attitude of management (one source, MLSU).
Organisational (poor relations) Faulty communication, dilution of supervision and command, non-recognition of trade unions, unfair practices, and violation of collective agreements, standing orders and labour laws.
Union-related The political nature of trade unions, multiple unions and inter-union rivalry weaken the trade union movement.
Psychological Lack of job security, poor organisational culture, non-recognition of merit and performance, authoritative administration and poor interpersonal relations.
Technological New technology, automation, mechanisation or computerisation introduced without proper consultation, preparation and discussion with workers.
Government Government-related factors are a listed category of causes (MLSU). Examples in college texts: change in economic policies and old labour laws; government machinery can itself become a cause.

Industrial democracy and the Webbs

  • The term comes from Sidney and Beatrice Webb, "Industrial Democracy" (London, 1897). There it means trade-union self-government and common-rule bargaining, not workers' sharing in managerial decisions. The book has no single defining sentence. The sense comes from the book's text and the ILO and Stanford accounts.
  • Sources differ on the meaning. Indian IR texts: "government mandated worker participation at various levels of the organisation with regard to decisions that affect workers" (LBRCE). The ILO says the term is used loosely, and that one view applies it only to powers that counter-balance management. The Stanford Encyclopedia says it denotes democratic organisation of unions and collective bargaining.
  • "Workers share in decision making" holds only for the Indian-text sense.

F.W. Taylor

  • In 1911 Taylor wrote that scientific management needs "a complete revolution in the mental attitude and the habits of all of those engaged in the management, as well of the workmen" (Principles of Scientific Management).
  • In 1912 he told the House Special Committee it involves "a complete mental revolution" on the part of the workingman and "the equally complete mental revolution" on the part of management. The 1912 text was read as an excerpt.
  • The exact phrase "mental revolution" is not in the 1911 book. It is a scientific-management idea.
  • Functional management replaces the single foreman by eight different men, each with his own special duties. Four are in the planning room. Four are shop bosses: gang bosses, speed bosses, inspectors and repair bosses. The four planning-room titles were not found in the opened text, so they are not named here.
